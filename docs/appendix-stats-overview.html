1.2 Test Statistics for Statistical Significance: Is It Real?

When you observe some interesting pattern in your data, you might wonder if it reflects a real effect in the population or just happened by chance. Null Hypothesis Significance Testing (NHST) answers this question by calculating the probability of observing data as extreme as yours or more extreme if there were no effect in the population (the null hypothesis). You can calculate this probability using a test statistic. The test statistics in Table 4 all estimate something about population parameters (means, variances, or relationships) and standardize those estimates by a reference unit, producing a value whose probability under the null hypothesis is known.

1.3 Effect Size for Practical Significance: Is It Meaningful?

When you observe some interesting pattern in your data, you might wonder whether it’s not just statistically real but also meaningful in practice. Effect size answers this by quantifying the strength of an effect in terms of the variability of the raw scores, independent of sample size, making it comparable across studies. You can assess this using standardized effect size metrics. The effect sizes in Table 8 all express an observed quantity relative to a reference unit, producing a value whose magnitude can be interpreted as small, medium, or large.

Footnotes

  1. Standard Error of the Mean (SEM) is the standard deviation of the sampling distribution of the mean. Because the variance of a sum of independent values is the sum of their variances, the variance of the mean is the population variance divided by the sample size. The SEM is therefore the square root of that: the population standard deviation divided by the square root of the sample size. When the population standard deviation is unknown, the sample standard deviation is used instead.↩︎
